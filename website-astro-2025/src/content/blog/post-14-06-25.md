**NOTE:** I use the terms "AI" and "LLM" sort of interchangeably. Partly because I still resent "AI" as a term and try to avoid it if possible. Please enjoy.

---

In 2025 two things seem to be simultaneously true. Depending on who you talk to "AI" is either largely overrated and janky - maybe good for certain niche tasks but mostly a waste of time... or... it's a form of revolutionary intelligence poised to overtake humanity. Where you sit on this spectrum probably comes down to how you view LLMs (Large Language Models(The tech behind what everyone calls "AI")) from an ethical perspective. There's lots to unpack there that's beyond the scope of this article, but those who deride the likes of Open AI for training their models on the sum total of accumulated human knowledge and then undermine the livelihoods of individuals whilst collecting huge piles of venture capital, have my sympathy. I think part of my worry around "AI" in general is that people are _overestimating_ the likelihood of them becoming sentient whilst _underestimating_ the damage that could be done removing the need for - to pull a number out of absolutely nowhere - 20% of the workforce. It's already happening. Companies are not built to resist the temptation AI brings. A human workforce is fundamentally a pain in the ass.

But despite they dizzying speed at which LLMs have been integrated into our lives there's still a lot they can't seem to master. Products such as the Humane AI Pin are almost to easy to lampoon but that product is emblematic of a type of "vapourware" that isn't exactly new, but it's highly typical of the current AI landscape. More than that - any time a product comes out that touts "agentic" AI you can almost guarantee it's going to be disappointing.

The point of this blog was to talk about Apple, so I'll get to it. My theory (I doubt it's original but nether the less) is that Apple is uniquely positioned when compared to other huge companies. And it's important to keep that in mind because just about every other mega-tech-company is heavily incentivised to lie through their teeth about AI. Google, Facebook, Amazon, Microsoft, Nvidia are all so deeply invested on AI now that they literally couldn't say a bad word about it publicly if they wanted to. But Apple is different. They don't make their money off software in the same way. Their bottom line depends still, largely on the sale of physical devices (mainly iPhones). So when a company like that looks at integration of an LLM, whether it's from Open AI or anyone else they look at it thinking "How is this thing going to affect the overall system on the device that we depend on for income". Not how are we going to directly make money off of this AI thing by shoving down our users throats. It's just a totally different set of incentives. I'm not saying they're a better company.

In [2024's WWDC ](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RXeOiIDNNek) Apple announced it was going to cram AI into as many nooks and crannies of their ecosystem as it could. There was a lot of big talk about deeply integrated agentic AI - the kind of AI that can take a direction from the user and go get shit done, independently and effectively. There was a now removed [ad](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IIOw69HztX0) with Bella Ramsey showing Siri pulling up the name of someone based off a location and vague timeframe "Who was the name of that guy I had a meeting with at Cafe Grenel a couple of months ago?" Doesn't seem like such a hard task right?

However over the next year Apple reneged and re-reneged so often it became a bit of a joke. At WWDC in 2025 most of the promised features were't there, with Apple stating it needed more time to [meet their quality standards](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NTLk53h7u_k). They're currently being [sued by shareholders](https://www.reuters.com/sustainability/boards-policy-regulation/apple-sued-by-shareholders-over-ai-disclosures-2025-06-20/) for allegedly overstating how much progress they had made in the AI department.

> "Apple shares have lost nearly one-fourth of their value since their December 26, 2024 record high, wiping out approximately $900 billion of market value."

So the market is punishing Apple for not acting quickly with AI and they're still dragging their feet. That genuinly makes me think that they saw how bad those features were going to be and decided taking the hit was better. That's pretty wild.

It seems like every time a company tries to make AI do something complex with multiple steps it fails. _Humane AI Pin_ Maybe we underestimate how hard it is to do that. Pulling somebodies name from a calendar event doesn't seem like much but doing it reliably, every single time, accounting for weird edge cases... that's probably a lot harder than it seems. And if something like that doesn't work reliably it is instantly the most irritating thing imaginable. In the same week as the relatively AI-less WWDC Apple published a [paper](https://ml-site.cdn-apple.com/papers/the-illusion-of-thinking.pdf) which helps explain their hand wringing.

The paper, titled "The Illusion of Thinking: Understanding the Strengths and Limitations of Reasoning Models via the Lens of Problem Complexity" suggests problems with the way Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) are measured. Suggesting that the emphasis on mathematical and coding benchmarks fail to assess the quality of a models "reasoning". Instead, the authors pit models against a series of puzzles such as the [Tower of Hanoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_of_Hanoi) and the [River Crossing Puzzle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_crossing_puzzle). They found that beyond a certain threshold of complexity their ability to reason their way through and solve problems tends to break down. "This behavior" the authors state "suggests a fundamental scaling limitation in the thinking capabilities of current reasoning models relative to problem complexity." The conclusion, although specifically regarding reasoning models could also be applied to other Large Language Models. Anyone who has watch an LLM lose the plot mid way through or hallucinate a solution knows how their brilliance can be equally matched by their stupidity.

> "This behavior suggests a fundamental scaling limitation in the thinking capabilities of current reasoning models relative to problem complexity." - Apple
 
So Apple may very well be worried that their ecosystem would be harmed by integrating these tools too deeply in their products. Their phones and devices enjoy a reputation of being a premium experience and they charge accordingly. Microsoft probably had concerns about CoPilot - but they they don't control the full stack of hardware and software in the same way as apple does - they weren't messing with a perception of unified quality. People expect some jank from Windows (I'm not saying jank doens't exist in the Apple ecosystem already - just look at Siri, but you take my point I hope.)

Anyway that's about it, I think. I could go off in a lot of different directions but I'm trying to keep my thoughts relatively contained. Basically, even if you think Apple is the Devil, when it comes to AI, it's worth taking note of what they do and say. Because there's a reality there that nobody else seems willing to admit.

I was reminded while writing this about a Computerphile video that talks about something similar. The limits of AI. Maybe not that, Just that it's not necessarily a given as we throw power and hardware at these language models that they'll improve. In fact it's arguable that they're close to as good as they'll ever be. Anyway, it's an interesting watch.
